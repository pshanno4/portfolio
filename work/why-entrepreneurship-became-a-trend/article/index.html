Why Entrepreneurship Became a Trend—and How Dropshipping Hype Leaves People Broke

A closer look at the gap between entrepreneurial hype and the systems required to build a durable business.

  • Published June 26, 2025
  • Reading time 4 min
  • Words 750

Scroll long enough and you’ll see it: the screenshots, the Shopify dashboards, the promises of five-figure months before you’ve even picked a product.

Dropshipping is everywhere. Print-on-demand, TikTok shops, Amazon side hustles. The new language of entrepreneurship is short-form, passive, and algorithm-optimized.

Everyone, it seems, is building a business.

But behind the scenes—off the feed, beyond the pitch deck—most of these “businesses” aren’t really businesses at all. They’re landing pages with high CPMs. They’re delayed shipments and chargebacks. They’re 2 AM “why isn’t [this] working?” Google searches and TikTok creators who quietly stop posting after three weeks.

Entrepreneurship has become a trend. And like most trends, it promises more than it delivers.

The Boom That Wasn’t Built to Last

Between 2021 and 2023, over 16 million new business applications were filed in the U.S.—a historic spike, outpacing even the post-recession recovery of 2010. Nearly half of those came from individuals under 35. Gen Z, in particular, has leaned into entrepreneurship not just as a career path, but as a kind of cultural identity. It’s aspirational. Aesthetic. Wrapped in freedom, self-ownership, and the idea that anyone with Wi-Fi and ambition can make it.

That intent is real. But the outcomes tell another story.

The average Shopify store processes fewer than 50 orders a year. Most dropshipping stores never record a single sale. And even among the successful ones, profit margins rarely break 20%—often much less after ad spend and refund losses. The data is clear: the majority of these “quick launch” models don’t scale, don’t last, and don’t pay.

Why Everyone's Doing It Anyway

It’s not hard to see the appeal. Platforms like TikTok and Instagram have made e-commerce look frictionless. The most viral business models are the ones that look easy—no inventory, no shipping, just clever marketing and a few clicks. Side hustle culture rewards speed over depth. And the platforms reward novelty over sustainability.

The result is a wave of entrepreneurs who aren’t really building businesses—they’re participating in a content loop. Consumption disguised as creation. “Passive income” as performance.

And dropshipping is the perfect vehicle:

You don’t need a product.

You don’t need capital.

You don’t need to care about what you’re selling.

You just need an audience. Or better—someone else’s to borrow.

The Reality Underneath

But here’s what those 60-second case studies don’t tell you:

Most dropshipping businesses fail. Not eventually—immediately. They fail because ad costs spike and no one converts. They fail because customers don’t wait 21 days for a knockoff product. They fail because their founders conflate revenue with profit, and don't realize the difference until the chargebacks start.

They also fail because platform policy changes break what seemed like working systems. TikTok suspends a store. Meta bans an ad account. Stripe holds funds in reserve. Suddenly, the $5,000 month becomes inaccessible—and the creator disappears.

More importantly, these businesses often weren’t designed to survive in the first place. They were built on the assumption that speed is more important than skill, and that visibility is the same as traction. They were never operationally sound. Just good at sounding like they might be.

What This Costs

For many, the damage isn’t just financial. It’s emotional.

These aren’t failed products. They’re failed identities. People go into these ventures with hope. They build stores and shoot videos and invest money they didn’t really have. When it doesn’t work, it doesn’t feel like a test. It feels like a personal flaw. Like maybe they just weren’t cut out for it.

Worse still, it distracts from skill-building that could have compounded. Time spent chasing TikTok trends could’ve gone into learning brand strategy. Into understanding customer experience. Into building something real.

Entrepreneurship, once a commitment, is now something you try. And when it doesn’t work in three months, you move on. But not before it takes something with you.

Where It Goes From Here

Entrepreneurship isn’t going away. Gen Z still leads the charge in business formation. Platform tools will keep making store-building easier. And creators will keep making it look fast.

But if we want any of this to be sustainable, the conversation has to shift. From speed to structure. From trend to foundation. From selling to serving.

You can’t skip the boring parts and expect to succeed.

You can’t automate what you don’t understand.

And you can’t build longevity from a template built for virality.

What comes next won’t be the next wave of dropshippers. It’ll be the people who stayed after the hype collapsed. Who learned the margins, wrote their own copy, talked to customers, and made something that wasn’t just fast—but true.

That’s entrepreneurship. Not as a trend. But as work.
