What Trade Show Booths Can Learn from Video Games

A case-led explanation of how participatory demos, social momentum, analytics, and iteration can turn a booth into an experience.

Project in 60 seconds

What it covers—and what Paul contributed.

Trade-show booths and video games compete for the same scarce resource: sustained attention. This Workinman article uses that connection to show event marketers how participation, short feedback loops, visible progress, social competition, analytics, and rapid iteration can turn a display into an experience.

Rather than relying on abstract advice, the article connects each principle to studio work and concrete use cases, including a virtual-reality product demonstration, a venue-wide scavenger hunt, live trivia, and an on-site projection adjustment.

Structure and audience

The piece was written for marketers, exhibit teams, and organizations that need to explain complex products in a busy physical environment.

Research and translation

Paul had to translate game-design language—attract mode, feedback loops, challenge pacing, progression, analytics—into operational guidance for event teams.

Collaboration

The article drew on Workinman’s project history and was developed with contributors who supplied examples, project context, outline direction, visual assets, and editorial review.

Structure and audience

The piece was written for marketers, exhibit teams, and organizations that need to explain complex products in a busy physical environment. Its structure follows the visitor journey: attract attention, make learning active, reward continued participation, create social momentum, capture useful behavior data, and adjust the experience when real conditions differ from the plan.

Research and translation

Paul had to translate game-design language—attract mode, feedback loops, challenge pacing, progression, analytics—into operational guidance for event teams. Carestream, Cisco Live, ExhibitorLive, and museum examples ground that language in problems prospective clients recognize.

Collaboration

The article drew on Workinman’s project history and was developed with contributors who supplied examples, project context, outline direction, visual assets, and editorial review. Paul’s role was to turn those inputs into a coherent B2B argument rather than a list of disconnected capabilities.

Paul’s role

Paul researched, structured, and wrote the article from Workinman’s project examples and editorial brief, incorporating review from the broader studio team.
