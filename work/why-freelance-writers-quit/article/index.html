Why 90% of Freelance Writers Quit—And How to Be the 10% Who Don’t

A practical look at the habits and business realities that drive freelancers out—and what makes a writing practice last.

  • Author Paul Shannon
  • Published June 5, 2025
  • Reading time 5 min
  • Words 922
About this projectView original publisher ↗

There’s a reason freelance writing feels so seductive from the outside. The promise is clean: write from anywhere, bill your worth, skip the commute, and own your time. And at first, it almost seems possible. You take a client or two. You put “Writer” in your bio. You wait for the momentum to build.

But for most, it never quite does. The inbox goes quiet. The pitches fade. The money runs thin. And soon, the dream collapses into something smaller—something quieter. A side gig. A seasonal hustle. Or a memory of a year you really tried.

Rough estimates suggest the majority of new freelancers exit within the first 12 to 24 months. While the often-quoted “90% quit” figure isn’t pinned to a single study, it tracks with broader patterns in creative labor. The number of freelance writers entering the field each year far outpaces the number building sustainable businesses. According to Upwork’s Freelance Forward 2023 report, 64 million Americans freelanced last year—an increase of four million from the year before.

But growth doesn't mean staying power. It just means people keep trying.

So why do most freelance writers quit? And more importantly—what do the ones who stay actually do differently?

The Five Quiet Killers of a Freelance Writing Career

1. Financial Insecurity, From Day One

The average freelance writer earns less than many think. Payoneer’s 2020 Freelancer Income Report estimated a global average of just $15–16 an hour. Even among U.S.-based writers, Make a Living Writing’s 2023 survey found that only a fraction broke past $60k per year. Most hover far lower—especially in their first 18 months. Without savings or a part-time cushion, many simply burn out financially before they ever stabilize.

2. The Client Drought

No one tells you that marketing yourself is a second full-time job. For new freelancers without referrals, the client pipeline runs dry fast. Freelancers Union reports that over a third of freelancers spend more time chasing work during slow periods. But many never adapt to that rhythm—they just assume they’ve failed, and leave.

3. Chasing Payments

Delayed invoices kill momentum. A study by Independent Economy Council showed that 72% of freelancers have dealt with late or missing invoices, with 59% being owed $50,000 or more for work. For writers relying on that income to pay rent or bills, the emotional cost of not being paid for finished work is sometimes enough to walk away.

4. Burnout & Isolation

Freelancing isn’t lonely until it is. Until you’re on your seventh pitch of the week with no response. Until no one claps when you land your biggest byline. A Muck Rack survey in 2024 found burnout to be a leading reason writers leave journalism altogether—a close proxy for freelance creatives. There’s no manager to protect your time. No colleagues to validate your wins. Just you, and the cursor, and the creeping doubt.

5. The Admin Avalanche

Being a freelancer means being your own legal team, finance department, scheduler, and sales funnel. It means tracking receipts, setting rates, chasing invoices, planning for taxes, buying your own healthcare, and negotiating contracts. Writers don’t quit because they can’t write. They quit because they didn’t know how much wasn’t writing.

Who Stays? What the 10% Do Differently

They aren’t always the best writers. But they’re almost always the most structured.

They build buffers. Most of the freelancers who make it long-term didn’t go all-in overnight. They saved six months of living expenses or started part-time. They had a strategy for riding the early instability, and they didn’t mistake slow months for failure.

They treat outreach like a system. The 10% don’t pitch when they’re desperate. They pitch every week, every quarter, regardless of how full their calendar looks. Most track their outreach in CRMs or spreadsheets, refining what works. Client acquisition is not an emergency measure—it’s an operating habit.

They set prices based on profit, not panic. Many who stay long-term have a target effective hourly rate, and reverse-engineer project pricing from it. They factor in admin time, expenses, and margin. They raise rates regularly. They decline lowball work not because they’re too good—but because it’s bad business.

They use contracts. Always. Writers who don’t get burned use contracts that include 50% deposits, late fees, kill fees, and clear scope. It’s not arrogance—it’s infrastructure. And it keeps them paid on time, every time.

They find community. Whether it’s an online peer group, a Slack workspace, or a shared spreadsheet of pitch calls, the freelancers who last rarely work in isolation.

They set real boundaries. The 10% block off weekends. They take time off. They use email signatures with office hours. They turn Slack off after 6. A healthy freelance career is not built on endless hustle—it’s built on permission to rest.

If You’re New: What to Do Next

Not everyone wants to freelance forever. But if you do—and you’re still early—your odds improve the moment you treat this not like a passion project, but like a business.

Start with your buffer. Save, or supplement, until you’re earning enough to survive without panic.

Systematize outreach. Send five pitches a week. Track them. Tweak and repeat.

Price like a business. Estimate your monthly income needs, then back-calculate your minimum rate per hour or deliverable.

Use contracts. Don’t skip this. Ever.

Invest in your skills. Not endlessly—but enough to stay sharp. One course. One event. One cohort-based program. Every few months.

And above all: build a circle. Find people who understand the loneliness. Who can help you charge more. Who can talk you out of quitting on the bad days.

You don’t have to be part of the 90%. But you do have to work like the 10%.

Because the ones who stay? They’re not just surviving. They’re running businesses. They’re growing. And they’re writing on their own terms.
